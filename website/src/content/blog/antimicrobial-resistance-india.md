An antibiotic is one of the few medicines that can lose its power for
everyone because of how it is used by anyone. Each time bacteria meet an
antibiotic, the few that happen to survive go on to multiply. Over time those
survivors take over, and the medicine that once cured an infection in two days
stops working. This is **antimicrobial resistance (AMR)**: germs no longer
respond to the medicines meant to kill them (WHO). It is not the patient who
becomes resistant. It is the bacteria, and they spread from person to person,
ward to ward and hospital to hospital.

For most of the last century we could rely on a newer, stronger antibiotic
when an older one failed. In Indian hospitals today, for some infections,
that next medicine is running out.

## How big is the problem in India

### What the hospital laboratories show

The Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) has run a national
surveillance network since 2013. Its 2024 report analysed **99,027
culture-positive bacterial isolates** from 21 regional centres. These are
tertiary care hospitals, and ICMR is careful to say the figures do not describe
infections in the community. They do describe what doctors in large
hospitals are up against.

- **E. coli**, the most common cause of urinary infections, is losing ground to
  carbapenems, a class long kept for serious cases. The share of E. coli
  isolates still killed by imipenem fell from 81.4% in 2017 to 57.6% in 2024.
- **Klebsiella pneumoniae**, a frequent cause of bloodstream infection and
  pneumonia, is worse. Imipenem susceptibility fell from 58.5% to 31.2% over the
  same years. Only about one in five isolates (20.3%) remained susceptible to
  cefotaxime, a common third-generation cephalosporin.
- **Acinetobacter baumannii**, a hospital pathogen of ventilated and very sick
  patients, was resistant to meropenem in 91.0% of isolates in 2024.
- **MRSA** (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus) rose from 33% of
  S. aureus isolates in 2017 to nearly 53% in 2024.

Network data from intensive care units point the same way. In a study of 26
tertiary hospitals and 89 ICUs between 2017 and 2018, carbapenem resistance was
found in 72% of hospital-acquired bloodstream infections caused by Klebsiella
and 77% of those caused by Acinetobacter. Central line-associated bloodstream
infection rates in neonatal ICUs exceeded 20 per 1,000 central line days
(Mathur et al., Lancet Global Health 2022).

### Deaths

The largest estimates come from the Global Research on Antimicrobial
Resistance (GRAM) studies published in The Lancet. They report two numbers:
deaths *attributable* to resistance (deaths that would not have occurred had
the infection been drug-sensitive) and deaths *associated* with it (deaths in
which a resistant infection was involved).

- Worldwide, bacterial AMR was attributable to 1.27 million deaths and
  associated with 4.95 million deaths in 2019 (Lancet, 2022).
- For 2021, the estimates were 1.14 million attributable and 4.71 million
  associated deaths globally. In South Asia, about 335,000 deaths were
  attributable and 1.26 million associated (Lancet, 2024).
- For India alone in 2021, the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation
  estimates 267,000 attributable and 987,000 associated deaths.
- Looking ahead, the 2024 study forecasts 39.1 million deaths attributable to
  AMR worldwide between 2025 and 2050, with **11.8 million of them in South
  Asia**, the highest of any region. South Asia is also forecast, with Latin
  America and the Caribbean, to have the highest all-age AMR death rate in 2050.

These are modelled estimates with wide uncertainty ranges. They are still the
best evidence we have, and every version so far has pointed in the same
direction.

The World Health Organization's surveillance system adds a regional view.
In 2023, about one in six laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections worldwide
was resistant to antibiotic treatment. In WHO's South-East Asia Region, which
includes India, the figure was one in three (WHO, October 2025).

## Why India is especially exposed

No single cause explains this. Several pressures act together.

**Antibiotics are easy to get.** Most antibiotics are Schedule H or H1 drugs
that should only be sold on a doctor's prescription. In practice the rule is
weakly enforced. In a study of 622 pharmacies in Delhi, Mumbai and Patna,
people trained to describe two to three weeks of tuberculosis-like symptoms,
with no prescription, were given antibiotics in 37% of visits (Satyanarayana
et al., Lancet Infectious Diseases 2016). An analysis of 2019 sales data
describes over-the-counter sales of most antibiotics as largely unrestricted
(Koya et al., 2022).

**We use the wrong mix.** India's per-person antibiotic use is not high by
world standards. What stands out is the type. In 2019, private-sector sales
came to 5,071 million defined daily doses. Watch-group antibiotics, the WHO
category with higher resistance risk, made up 54.9% of that, and Access
antibiotics only 27.0%. Formulations without central regulatory approval
accounted for 47.1% (Koya et al., 2022). In a national survey at 20 tertiary hospitals in 2021–22, 71.9% of inpatients were on antibiotics. Only 6%
were on definitive (culture-directed) therapy; 94% were on empirical
therapy, and 55% received antibiotics for prophylaxis rather than treatment
(NAP-AMR 2.0, 2025).

**A high burden of infection.** Where diarrhoea, typhoid, pneumonia and
crowded wards are common, more antibiotics are used, and more resistant germs
spread. Hospitals are part of this: device-related infections in ICUs are
both a source of resistant bacteria and a reason for heavy antibiotic use.

**Animals and food.** Antibiotics are used in poultry, livestock and fish
farming. A 2015 global model forecast that antimicrobial use in food animals
would grow 67% worldwide between 2010 and 2030, and 99% in Brazil, Russia,
India, China and South Africa (Van Boeckel et al., PNAS 2015). India has since
acted: in 2019 the government prohibited colistin for food-producing animals,
poultry, aquaculture and animal feed supplements (NAP-AMR 2.0).

**Manufacturing waste.** India makes a large share of the world's antibiotics.
In 2016, researchers sampled water near bulk drug factories, sewage treatment
plants and the Musi river around Hyderabad. All samples from 28 sites contained
antimicrobials, and resistant bacteria, including carbapenemase producers,
were widespread (Lübbert et al., Infection 2017). The authors linked this to
poor wastewater management by the plants.

## What India is doing

India has been working on AMR for over a decade, and the policy base is
better than many people realise.

- **The Chennai Declaration (2012).** Indian medical societies met in Chennai
  in August 2012 and agreed a roadmap for tackling resistance, later published
  as a five-year plan (Ghafur et al., 2014).
- **Schedule H1 and the red line.** Under the Drugs Rules, 1945, 24 higher-end
  antimicrobials are listed in Schedule H1, and pharmacies must keep records of
  their sale for three years. The Ministry of Health's "Red Line" campaign asks
  the public not to take any medicine with a red vertical line on the pack
  unless a doctor has prescribed it (PIB, 2024).
- **National Action Plans.** The first National Action Plan on AMR ran from
  2017 to 2021. The second, **NAP-AMR 2.0 (2025–2029)**, was launched on
  18 November 2025. It gives each participating ministry its own plan with
  timelines and budgets, and aims to strengthen ownership, coordination
  between sectors and private-sector engagement (PIB, 2025).
- **ICMR stewardship guidance.** In 2018 ICMR published an Antimicrobial
  Stewardship Program Guideline to help hospitals set up their own programmes.
  Its preface notes plainly that most Indian hospitals lacked the structure and
  process for stewardship.
- **Kerala.** In 2018 Kerala became the first state to release its own
  action plan, KARSAP, drafted jointly by the health, animal husbandry,
  environment, fisheries and agriculture departments. Its work includes a state
  surveillance network, a drive to make primary health centres
  "antibiotic smart", and a
  culture-report format that groups results by AWaRe category, developed at
  Government Medical College, Thiruvananthapuram (ASHE, 2025).
- **AWaRe.** WHO's AWaRe system sorts antibiotics into Access, Watch and
  Reserve groups. WHO set a target of at least 60% Access use, and the 2024 UN
  political declaration on AMR raised the global goal to
  70% by 2030. India has some way to go: in the national hospital survey above,
  38% of antibiotic prescriptions were Access and 57% Watch. A 2023 review also
  noted that the National List of Essential Medicines 2022 does not group its
  antibiotics by AWaRe category (Manikandan, 2023).

## What hospitals can do

Hospitals cannot fix pharmacy sales or factory effluent. They can control
what happens inside their own walls, and the evidence that this works is
strong.

### Run a real stewardship programme

A Cochrane review of 221 studies found that stewardship interventions for
hospital inpatients raised the share of patients treated according to
antibiotic policy from 43% to 58%. They shortened antibiotic courses by 1.95
days, from an average of 11.0 days, without any difference in deaths (11% in
both groups), and probably reduced hospital stay by about a day. Interventions
that included feedback to prescribers worked better (Davey et al., 2017).

ICMR's guideline describes the core team: a physician trained in infectious
diseases, a clinical microbiologist and a clinical pharmacist, working with
infection control staff and backed by hospital leadership. The team needs
protected time, not goodwill alone.

### The core practices

These steps appear in ICMR's stewardship guideline and are well within reach
of most hospitals:

1. **Culture before antibiotics.** Send blood, urine or other cultures before
   the first dose wherever it is safe to wait. Without a culture, the team
   cannot de-escalate later, and 94% empirical therapy becomes 100%.
2. **Know your own bugs.** Publish a hospital antibiogram at least once a year,
   ideally split by ICU and ward, so empirical choices reflect local resistance
   rather than habit.
3. **Build the formulary around AWaRe.** Make Access antibiotics the default
   for common infections. Require a reason, or approval, for Watch drugs, and
   restrict Reserve drugs such as colistin to cases agreed with the
   stewardship team.
4. **Review at 48 to 72 hours.** Once culture results arrive, narrow the
   antibiotic, switch to a single drug, or stop it if there is no infection.
5. **Switch from IV to oral** when the patient can swallow and is improving,
   using drugs that are well absorbed by mouth. It also lowers the risk of
   complications from IV lines.
6. **Set durations.** Write a stop date on every antibiotic order, and keep
   surgical prophylaxis short rather than letting it drift into days of
   treatment.
7. **Audit and feed back.** Measure antibiotic use, share results with each
   department, and repeat. Feedback is one of the better-proven parts of the
   evidence base.

### Stop infections before they start

Every infection prevented is a course of antibiotics never given. Hand
hygiene at the right moments, care bundles for central lines, urinary catheters
and ventilators, and isolation of patients carrying resistant organisms are
the backbone of infection prevention. NAP-AMR 2.0 names infection control in
healthcare facilities as a key strategy, and the 2024 UN declaration set a
goal for 90% of countries to meet WHO's minimum requirements for infection
prevention and control programmes by 2030.

### Let the system help

Much of this depends on information reaching the right person at the right
moment. A hospital information system can carry part of the load: showing the
AWaRe group on every antibiotic order, prompting for a culture before the first
dose, asking for a stop date, and turning laboratory results into a regular
antibiogram. MedBrains, the open-standards hospital information system we
build, records the AWaRe group for each antibiotic in the hospital formulary
and reports an antibiogram of resistance by ward and organism; the rest is on
our list. The principle applies to any system: make the right choice the easy
one.

## What patients and families can do

- **Do not ask for antibiotics for a cold, flu or most viral fevers.**
  Antibiotics act on bacteria, not viruses. If your doctor says you do not need
  one, that is good care, not neglect.
- **Take them exactly as prescribed**, at the dose and for the length of time
  your doctor advises. Ask if you are unsure how long to continue.
- **Never share antibiotics or use leftovers**, and do not buy them without a
  prescription. Look for the red line on the pack.
- **Keep vaccinations up to date.** WHO estimates that vaccines against 24
  pathogens could cut global antibiotic use by 22%, about 2.5 billion daily
  doses a year (WHO, 2024).
- **Wash your hands and ask others to.** In hospital, it is reasonable to ask
  a doctor or nurse whether they have cleaned their hands before touching you
  or your family member.

## A closing word

Antimicrobial resistance does not arrive as a single emergency. It arrives as
a urinary infection that needs a third antibiotic, a newborn whose bloodstream
infection responds to nothing on the shelf, a routine operation that becomes
riskier each year. The numbers above are serious, but they are not fixed.
The 2024 GRAM study estimates that better care for severe infections and
better access to the right antibiotics could avert 92 million deaths worldwide
between 2025 and 2050. Much of that work happens in ordinary hospitals, one
prescription and one washed pair of hands at a time.
