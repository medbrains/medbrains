HYROX is growing fast. The fitness race, which its
organisers say drew more than 550,000 athletes across 80-plus races
worldwide in 2025, has upcoming events listed in Gujarat (26 to 28
November 2026), Noida (15 to 17 January 2027) and Bengaluru (19 to 23 May
2027, at the Bangalore International Exhibition Centre). Many people
signing up are attempting their first race.

That is good news for the nation's fitness. It also means more sore shins,
twisted ankles and calves that "just feel tight". This guide explains what
the race asks of your lower legs, why injuries happen, how to build up over
8 to 12 weeks, and the warning signs that mean you should stop and see a
doctor.

## What HYROX asks of your lower legs

Every HYROX race follows the same format: you run 1 km, then complete one
workout station, eight times over. In the Open division the stations are:

| Station | What you do | Open load (women / men) |
|---|---|---|
| SkiErg | 1,000&nbsp;m | none |
| Sled push | 2&nbsp;×&nbsp;25&nbsp;m | 102&nbsp;kg / 152&nbsp;kg, including the sled |
| Sled pull | 2&nbsp;×&nbsp;25&nbsp;m | 78&nbsp;kg / 103&nbsp;kg, including the sled |
| Burpee broad jumps | 80&nbsp;m | bodyweight |
| Rowing | 1,000&nbsp;m | none |
| Farmers carry | 200&nbsp;m | 2&nbsp;×&nbsp;16&nbsp;kg / 2&nbsp;×&nbsp;24&nbsp;kg |
| Sandbag lunges | 100&nbsp;m | 10&nbsp;kg / 20&nbsp;kg |
| Wall balls | 100&nbsp;reps | 4&nbsp;kg / 6&nbsp;kg |

Look at it from the point of view of your shin bone (tibia), the thin
outer bone beside it (fibula) and your ankle. You run 8 km in total. You
drive a heavy sled with your calves and ankles bent forward. You jump 80 m.
You lunge 100 m while carrying a load. You squat and jump to throw 100 wall
balls. Very little of the race lets the lower leg rest.

The first research on HYROX athletes bears this out. A University of Bath
study followed 89 HYROX athletes for 12 weeks. In any given week, 30% of
them reported some health problem. Of the injuries, 73% were overuse
injuries, not sudden accidents, and the knee and lower leg carried the
greatest burden. The authors linked this to the sport's running load.

## The common lower-leg injuries

### Shin splints (medial tibial stress syndrome)

An aching pain along the inner edge of the shin that comes on with running or jumping. The NHS lists the usual triggers:
starting exercise after a period of inactivity, running or jumping on hard
surfaces, and poor running technique. Most cases settle within a few weeks
with rest and a gradual return.

### Stress fractures of the tibia and fibula

Bone is living tissue that constantly repairs tiny cracks caused by
training. When load outpaces repair, the bone fails in stages: first a
stress reaction, then a stress fracture, and if you keep going, a complete
break. Bone stress injuries also tend to come back, which is why the
underlying cause needs fixing, not just the pain.

The tell-tale signs are pain at one small spot on the bone that you can
press with a fingertip, pain that builds with every session, and sometimes
swelling. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons notes that most stress
fractures take 6 to 8 weeks to heal, and that returning too early can turn
a stress fracture into a complete one.

### Achilles tendon pain

Pain and stiffness in the cord at the back of the heel, typically during
the first steps of the day, coming on gradually. NHS physiotherapy guidance
puts it down to repetitive stress and tiny injuries to the tendon. Sled
pushes, burpee broad jumps and running all load the Achilles hard.

### Ankle sprains

A rolled ankle on a lunge, a jump landing or an uneven pavement during a
training run. A sprain is an injury to the ligaments that hold the ankle
together. The Dutch evidence-based guideline published in the *British
Journal of Sports Medicine* says the severity of ligament damage is judged
most reliably 4 to 5 days after the injury, once the first swelling has
settled.

### Calf strains

A sudden pull or sharp pain in the calf, often when pushing off hard. Tired
calves late in a race, after the sled push and with several kilometres still
to run, are at particular risk.

## Why these injuries happen

**Doing too much, too soon.** This is the most common cause of stress
fractures, according to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, and it
catches experienced athletes too. Typical examples: signing up for a race
eight weeks away and doubling your running, or adding sled work and
running sessions in the same week.

**The sled and the lunges.** Pushing a sled keeps your ankles bent forward
and your calves working hard for every step. Lunges and wall balls add
hundreds of loaded knee and ankle bends. Each one is fine on its own. The
problem is adding them on top of a big running week.

**Hard surfaces and worn shoes.** Running on concrete roads, changing
surface suddenly (from treadmill to road, for example) and shoes that have
lost their cushioning all raise the risk of stress fractures.

**Not eating enough.** The International Olympic Committee's 2023 consensus
statement describes Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs): when you
train hard without eating enough to cover the work, the body cuts back on
other systems, including bone health. It affects men as well as women, and
it is common among people who are training for a race while also trying to
lose weight.

**Low vitamin D.** A review by doctors at AIIMS, New Delhi, found that
studies in India report vitamin D deficiency in 40% to 99% of people, with
most finding 80% to 90%, across all age groups. Low vitamin D is linked to
stress fractures. Sunshine alone does not guarantee good levels, especially
for people who train indoors or early in the morning.

**A previous injury.** In the HYROX study, a previous injury was the
strongest possible risk marker for how severe new injuries were, though the
study was too small to be sure. If you have had shin or ankle trouble
before, build up more slowly.

## An 8 to 12 week build-up that respects your legs

There is no single perfect plan, and if you are new to running or returning
from injury you should allow longer. These principles come from the
evidence.

### 1. Increase slowly, and treat the 10% rule as a ceiling

You may have heard that you should never add more than 10% a week. The
picture is more nuanced. In a Dutch trial of 532 novice runners, a 13-week
plan built on the 10% rule had almost exactly the same injury rate as a
standard 8-week plan: 20.8% against 20.3%. A Danish study of 874 new runners
suggested the real danger is a big jump: those who increased their weekly
distance by more than 30% over two weeks appeared more prone to injuries
such as shin splints than those who stayed under 10%.

In practice:

- **Build in small steps.** Increase either running distance or station
  work in a given week, not both.
- **Take lighter weeks** regularly, so your bones and tendons can catch up.
- **Count everything.** Sled pushes, lunges and burpee broad jumps are leg
  load, not "just gym".

### 2. Make strength training non-negotiable

This is the best-supported injury prevention there is. A meta-analysis of
25 trials covering 26,610 people, published in the *British Journal of
Sports Medicine*, found that strength training cut sports injuries to
about a third, and that injury prevention programmes almost halved overuse
injuries. Stretching on its own made no measurable difference.

Short, regular sessions are enough. Focus on:

- **Calves.** Slow single-leg calf raises, with the knee straight and
  then bent, are the base. Strengthening the calf is specifically
  recommended to protect the tibia against bone stress injuries.
- **The front of the shin (tibialis anterior).** Toe raises with your back
  against a wall.
- **Hips and thighs.** Split squats, step-ups, single-leg deadlifts and
  side planks keep your knees and ankles lined up when you are tired.
- **Feet and balance.** Single-leg balance, eyes open and then closed.
  Balance training reduced injuries in the same meta-analysis.

### 3. Add jumping gradually

Burpee broad jumps and wall balls are both jumps. Start with low-level
hops and small broad jumps on a forgiving surface, focus on landing quietly
with your knee over your toes, and build up the number of contacts week by
week. A softer, quieter landing and a slightly quicker running stride are
both suggested ways to reduce impact on the shin.

### 4. Practise the race, a piece at a time

Do short "compromised running" sessions: a station followed by a 1 km run
at race pace. Only add a full race simulation once you have done the pieces
comfortably, and do your last long simulation well before race day, not in
the final days.

### 5. Sleep, rest days and food

- **Sleep and rest days** are when bone and tendon adapt. Plan at least
  full rest days into every week.
- **Eat enough for the work you do.** Training for HYROX is not the time
  for a crash diet.
- **Protein** at every meal: dal, paneer, curd, eggs, fish, chicken, soya
  or sprouts.
- **Calcium** from milk, curd, paneer, ragi and leafy greens.
- **Iron**, especially for women and vegetarians: green leafy vegetables,
  pulses and jaggery, ideally with a source of vitamin C such as lemon or
  amla.
- **Vitamin D**: ask your doctor whether a blood test is worth doing before
  a hard training block, and take supplements only as advised.

## Race-day tips

- **Do not try anything new.** No new shoes, no new food, no new drink.
- **Wear shoes you have trained in,** with grip that suits the sled and the
  course. Replace worn shoes weeks before the race, not the night before.
- **Warm up properly.** Easy jogging, leg swings, calf raises,
  a few lunges and some short build-up runs.
- **Pace the first kilometre.** It is easy to get excited and run the first
  1 km far too fast. The legs pay for it at the sled.
- **Hydrate for the conditions.** Venues can be hot and humid. The
  American College of Sports Medicine advises preventing a loss of more than
  2% of body weight through sweat during exercise. Weigh yourself before and
  after a training session in similar conditions to learn how much you sweat.
  Drink to that plan, and use an electrolyte drink if you sweat heavily.
  Know the warning signs of heat illness: our guide to
  [heat stroke first aid](/blog/heat-stroke-first-aid) explains them.
- **Stop if something feels wrong.** A finish time is never worth a
  fracture.

## Apps and tools that can help

You do not need to buy anything special, but a few free or simple tools make
safe training easier:

- **A training log.** A notebook or spreadsheet works. Write down weekly
  running distance, station work and a pain score from 0 to 10 for your
  shins, ankles and calves. A rising pain score is your early warning.
- **A GPS watch or phone running app.** Useful for seeing your true weekly
  distance, so you can spot a jump before it hurts you. The Danish running
  study used GPS watches for exactly this.
- **The Fit India app.** The Ministry of Youth Affairs and Sports launched
  it on 29 August 2021. It is free, works in English and Hindi, and gives a
  fitness score from age-specific tests, with guidance on improving it.
- **Your ABHA health record.** If you do have an X-ray or MRI, keep the
  report linked to your ABHA so any doctor you see later can find it.
  [Our guide to ABHA](/blog/abha-health-id-explained) explains how.
- **The official HYROX website** for the exact race format, standards and
  event details. Training plans are everywhere online; prefer ones that
  include rest weeks and strength work.

## Red flags: when to stop and see a doctor

**Stop running and see a doctor soon if you have:**

- **pain at one small spot on the shin or foot bone** that you can point to
  with a fingertip;
- **pain at rest or at night,** or pain that gets worse with every session
  instead of easing as you warm up;
- **swelling** over the shin, ankle or the top of the foot;
- **shin, ankle or Achilles pain** that is getting worse, or not improving
  despite reduced training and self-care.

**For a twisted ankle,** doctors use the Ottawa ankle rules to decide
whether you need an X-ray. A systematic review of 27 studies with 15,581
patients found the rules pick up almost 100% of fractures. In plain words,
an X-ray is usually needed if:

- you could not take four steps on the foot straight after the injury, and
  still cannot when you are seen; or
- it hurts when the doctor presses on the back edge or tip of either ankle
  bone (the bony bumps on each side); or
- it hurts when pressed on the bone at the middle of the inner edge of
  the foot, or at the bony bump halfway along the outer edge of the foot.

For a mild sprain, the NHS advises protecting the ankle, resting it, ice
wrapped in a cloth for up to 20 minutes every 2 to 3 hours, a compression
bandage and keeping it raised for the first 2 to 3 days. After that, a
structured exercise programme does more for recovery than rest alone, and
an ankle brace can help prevent the next sprain.

**Go to a hospital emergency department straight away if:**

- you heard a crack or a snap, or the leg or ankle looks out of shape;
- you cannot put any weight on the leg;
- you feel sudden, sharp pain at the back of the heel, sometimes with a
  snap you can feel or hear, which can mean a torn Achilles tendon;
- the foot is numb, tingling, cold, or pale or blue;
- **one calf is throbbing, painful or swollen, with red or darker skin,**
  especially after a journey of more than four hours by plane, train or car
  to reach a race. This can be a blood clot (deep vein thrombosis);
- you are short of breath or have chest pain with or after calf pain. Call
  112. A clot that has travelled to the lungs is life-threatening.

## The bottom line

HYROX is a great goal, and almost every lower-leg injury it causes is
preventable. Build up slowly, count the sled and the lunges as leg work,
strength-train your calves, shins and hips, eat enough, and
check your vitamin D. Listen to pinpoint bone pain. It is your body telling
you to rest before a small crack becomes a broken bone.

*This article is general health information, not medical advice. If you
have pain or an injury, see a doctor or physiotherapist. In an emergency,
call 112 or go to the nearest hospital.*
