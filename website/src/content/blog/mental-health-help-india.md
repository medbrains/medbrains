> **If you or someone near you is in danger right now,** call **112** or go to
> the nearest hospital emergency department. To talk to a trained mental
> health counsellor at any hour, call **Tele-MANAS on 14416 or
> 1-800-891-4416**. The call is free.

On 10 October, India marked World Mental Health Day. This year's global
theme, which the Union Health Ministry adopted for its observance, is "Lived
experiences heard: real voices, real change": a reminder that the people who
know most about mental illness are the people who have lived through it.

The ministry's figures show how many people are now reaching out. Tele-MANAS,
the national tele-mental health helpline launched on 10 October 2022, had
received 47,75,644 calls by October 2026, through 53 cells across all 36
states and union territories. Of the 13,35,275 callers whose profile was
recorded, 73.2% were between 18 and 45 years old: students, young workers,
new parents, people in the busiest years of their lives.

That is encouraging. It also means that, in many Indian homes, someone is
quietly struggling and the people around them are not sure what to do. This
guide is for them: the parent, the spouse, the friend, the room-mate.

## How common is this, really?

Far more common than most families assume.

The National Mental Health Survey, run by NIMHANS across 12 states in
2015–16, found that about 10.6% of adults had a mental health condition at
the time of the survey, and 13.7% had had one at some point in their lives.
A national analysis published in *The Lancet Psychiatry* estimated that 197.3
million people in India were living with a mental disorder in 2017, including
45.7 million with depression and 44.9 million with anxiety disorders.

Most of them were not getting care. The same survey found that, depending on
the condition, between 70% and 92% of people who needed treatment were not
receiving it. Stigma, distance, cost and simply not recognising the signs all
play a part.

The good news is that these are treatable conditions. The World Health
Organization is clear that there are effective treatments for mild, moderate
and severe depression, including talking therapies and, where needed,
medicines.

## Signs that someone may need support

Everyone has bad days, bad weeks, sad moods after a loss. What matters is a
change that lasts and that gets in the way of ordinary life. WHO describes a
depressive episode as a low, irritable or empty mood, or a loss of interest
and pleasure, lasting most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two
weeks. Other signs include:

- poor concentration, or falling behind at studies or work;
- strong feelings of guilt or worthlessness;
- hopelessness about the future;
- sleeping far more or far less than usual;
- changes in appetite or weight;
- feeling very tired or low in energy all the time;
- thoughts about dying or suicide.

In everyday life this often looks like someone withdrawing from friends,
skipping classes or work, not answering calls, giving up things they used to
enjoy, or becoming unusually irritable. Some people simply go quiet.

Depression is also common around childbirth. WHO notes that more than 10% of
pregnant women and new mothers worldwide experience it. Under the National
Health Mission, ASHAs and ANMs screen new mothers for postpartum depression
during their postnatal home visits, so a mother who is struggling can ask her
ASHA for help.

### Warning signs that need you to act today

Some signs mean the person may be at risk of harming themselves. Take them
seriously, every time:

- talking or writing about dying, suicide or not wanting to live;
- deep hopelessness about the future;
- a previous suicide attempt, which WHO identifies as an important risk
  factor;
- a recent crisis such as financial trouble, a relationship breaking down,
  a loss, or long-term pain or illness, especially in someone already
  struggling;
- growing isolation, which WHO notes is strongly associated with suicidal
  behaviour.

WHO also notes that many suicides happen impulsively, in a moment of crisis.
That is exactly why the people nearby matter so much.

## How to help someone you are worried about

You do not need to be an expert. You need to be present, calm and willing to
listen.

**1. Choose a quiet moment and ask.** "You haven't seemed yourself lately.
I'm worried about you. How are you really doing?" Then let them talk.

**2. If you are worried about suicide, ask directly.** "Are you thinking
about ending your life?" Many people fear this question will put the idea in
someone's head. The evidence says it does not: a review of published studies
in *Psychological Medicine* found none that showed a significant increase in
suicidal thoughts after people were asked, and suggested that talking about
it may reduce them. Asking tells the person it is safe to be honest.

**3. Listen more than you speak.** Avoid "think positive", "others have it
worse" or "just pray more". You do not have to fix anything in the first
conversation. "Thank you for telling me" is often enough.

**4. Do not keep a serious risk secret.** If someone tells you they plan to
harm themselves, you cannot promise to keep that to yourself. Tell them you
care too much to do that, and bring in help.

**5. Help them take the next step.** Offer to sit with them while they call
Tele-MANAS, or to go with them to a doctor. A practical offer ("I'll come
with you on Saturday") works better than "you should see someone".

**6. Stay close if the risk is high.** Do not leave the person alone. Keep
them away from anything they could use to harm themselves, such as stored
medicines or pesticides. WHO lists limiting access to such means among the
most effective ways to prevent suicide.

**7. Keep checking in.** Recovery takes time. A message a few days later
("Thinking of you, want to meet for chai?") tells someone they have not been
forgotten.

**8. Look after yourself too.** Supporting someone is tiring. Share the load
with others you trust, and seek support for yourself if you need it.

## Where to get help in India

**Tele-MANAS: 14416 or 1-800-891-4416.** Free, 24 hours a day, every day.
Calls are answered first by trained counsellors and can be escalated to
psychiatrists, clinical psychologists, psychiatric social workers and
psychiatric nurses, with referral to in-person care through the District
Mental Health Programme. The service runs in 20 languages, depending on the
languages each state has chosen. There is also a Tele-MANAS mobile app, now
available in English, Hindi and 10 regional languages, with a design that
supports visually impaired users, and a video consultation facility.

**Your nearest government hospital or health centre.** The District Mental
Health Programme has been sanctioned in 767 districts, with outpatient care,
counselling, medicines and a 10-bed inpatient facility at district level.
Mental health services have also been added to the primary care package at
the Ayushman Arogya Mandirs (upgraded sub-health and primary health centres).
There are 47 government-run mental hospitals, and mental health services are
available at all AIIMS.

**Manodarpan, for students and teachers: 8448440632.** The Ministry of
Education's psychosocial support helpline offers tele-counselling by trained
psychologists.

**In an emergency: 112.** India's single emergency number connects you to
police, fire and medical help.

**Cost should not be a barrier.** Under Ayushman Bharat PM-JAY, cashless
treatment is available for 22 specified mental health procedures. And under
the Mental Healthcare Act, people living below the poverty line, or who are
destitute or homeless, are entitled to free mental health treatment at
government-run or government-funded facilities.

## Your rights under the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017

India's mental health law is built around the dignity and choices of the
person receiving care. Some of the rights it gives are worth knowing before
you need them:

- **Right to access care (section 18).** Every person has the right to
  affordable, good-quality mental health care from government-run or funded
  services, without discrimination on grounds including gender, sexual
  orientation, religion, caste, class or disability. Medicines on the
  essential drug list are to be free at government facilities from community
  health centres upwards.
- **Right to confidentiality (section 23).** Information about a person's
  mental health and treatment must be kept confidential, with limited
  exceptions such as sharing with the treating team or preventing harm. This
  applies to digital records too. No photograph or information about a
  person in treatment may be released to the media without their consent
  (section 24).
- **Advance directive (section 5).** Any adult can write down in advance how
  they wish, and do not wish, to be cared for if they become unwell.
- **Nominated representative (section 14).** Any adult can name, in writing
  on plain paper, a person to support them in decisions about their care.
- **Right to live in the community and with dignity (sections 19 and 20).**
  No one should remain in a mental health facility merely because their
  family will not take them home, and everyone in care is protected from
  cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment.
- **Equal insurance cover (section 21).** Insurers must provide medical
  insurance for mental illness on the same basis as for physical illness.
- **Free legal aid (section 27).** A person with mental illness is entitled
  to free legal services to exercise these rights.

And one provision every family should know: **attempting suicide is not
treated as a crime.** Section 115 says a person who attempts suicide is
presumed to have been under severe stress and shall not be tried and
punished. Instead, the government has a duty to provide care, treatment and
rehabilitation. No one should be afraid to bring a loved one to hospital
after a self-harm attempt.

## When to seek help, and when to go to hospital

**Talk to a doctor or call Tele-MANAS soon if:**

- low mood, worry, irritability or loss of interest has lasted two weeks or
  more;
- sleep, appetite, studies, work or relationships are clearly suffering;
- someone is using alcohol or other substances to cope;
- a new mother seems persistently low, anxious or unable to bond with her
  baby;
- an older relative has become withdrawn, confused or stopped eating well.

**Go to a hospital emergency department, or call 112, at once if someone:**

- has harmed themselves, or has taken an overdose of any medicine or a
  poison, even if they seem well now;
- talks about a plan to end their life, or you believe they may act on it;
- is hearing voices telling them to harm themselves or others;
- is extremely agitated, confused or out of touch with reality and cannot
  be kept safe at home.

Stay with them, keep them away from anything they could use to harm
themselves, and let the hospital team know what you have seen.

## The bottom line

Mental health conditions are common in India, treatable, and still too often
hidden. The steps that help most are simple: notice the change, ask
honestly, listen without judging, and help the person take the next step
towards care. Recovery is possible, and many people who have been through a
crisis go on to live full lives.

If this article has stirred something difficult for you, you do not have to
carry it alone. Call Tele-MANAS on 14416 at any hour.

*This article is general health information, not medical advice. In an
emergency, call 112 or go to the nearest hospital.*
