The consultant finishes the morning round and says the patient can go home
today. The family calls for the car. Then the afternoon passes. The bed stays
occupied, the patient waiting in casualty for it waits too, and the family walks
out in the early evening.

Very little of that time is clinical. It goes on paperwork, queues and hand-offs
the hospital controls, which means the hospital can win it back.

## What the regulator now expects of insurers

The Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India (IRDAI) issued its
Master Circular on Health Insurance Business on 29 May 2024 (reference
IRDAI/HLT/CIR/PRO/84/5/2024). Two of its rules bear directly on the discharge
desk.

- **Cashless authorisation.** An insurer must decide on a request for cashless
  authorisation immediately, and in no more than one hour of receiving it.
  Insurers were given until 31 July 2024 to put the systems in place.
- **Final discharge authorisation.** An insurer must grant final authorisation
  within three hours of receiving the discharge authorisation request from the
  hospital. The circular says the policyholder should not be made to wait to be
  discharged. If the insurer takes longer than three hours, any additional
  amount the hospital charges is to be borne by the insurer from its
  shareholders' funds, not by the patient.
- **Death during treatment.** The insurer must process the claim immediately and
  get the body released from the hospital immediately.

The circular also says that once a claim is intimated, insurers and third-party
administrators (TPAs) should collect the documents they need from the hospital,
and the policyholder should not be asked to submit them.

Read the three-hour rule carefully. The clock starts when the insurer receives
the hospital's request, not when the doctor writes "fit for discharge". Every
minute spent finishing the summary, closing the bill and assembling the file
before the request goes out sits on the hospital's side of the ledger.

The circular is to be reviewed every year, so check the IRDAI website for later
changes before rewriting your own policy.

## What NABH expects of hospitals

The National Accreditation Board for Hospitals and Healthcare Providers (NABH)
published its 6th edition standards for hospitals, effective 1 January 2025. Two
standards in the chapter on access, assessment and continuity of care cover
discharge.

Standard AAC.12 asks that discharge is planned with the patient or family,
coordinated across the departments involved, and that every patient leaving,
including those leaving against medical advice, receives a discharge summary.
The hospital should adhere to planned discharges. One element has become
stricter: monitoring discharge time, setting benchmarks and improving on them
was an "excellence" element in the 5th edition and is now a "commitment"
element, which NABH assesses at the hospital's final assessment.

Standard AAC.13 covers the summary itself: standardised content, follow-up
advice and medicines in language the patient can understand, instructions on
when and how to get urgent care, and the cause of death where a patient has
died. NABH's glossary describes a summary as covering the reasons for admission,
significant findings, procedures, treatment, the patient's condition at
discharge and instructions given to the family.

The 6th edition also lists "time taken for discharge" among its indicators,
collected monthly in minutes. The clock starts when the consultant formally
approves discharge and stops when the patient leaves the clinical unit. Extra
time the patient asks for is not counted, and day-care patients are left out.

You will often see a figure of 180 minutes described as the NABH standard for
discharge in Indian studies. The 6th edition text we read asks each hospital to
set appropriate benchmarks rather than fixing a single national number. Three
hours is a reasonable internal target, but it is yours to set.

## Where the hours actually go

Indian discharge studies are mostly single-hospital audits with wide variation,
so treat them as pointers, not national averages. Together they keep naming the
same culprits.

**AIIMS Bhubaneswar.** An audit of four major wards in June 2018 found a mean
discharge time of 195 minutes. Billing clearance took the largest share, around
half the process, followed by writing the discharge summary. About a third of
discharges were held up by more than an hour at billing. Obstetrics and
gynaecology discharged faster than general surgery, partly because summaries
were drafted the night before a planned discharge. Many families had not been
told in advance what the billing process needed, so they were not ready when the
bill arrived (Sahoo et al., 2019).

**SKIMS, Srinagar.** Observing 710 discharges from medicine and surgery wards
between November 2017 and January 2018, researchers found an average of 240
minutes for planned, self-paying patients, 255 minutes for patients discharged
against medical advice, and 270 minutes for below-poverty-line patients who
needed charges waived (Hamid et al., 2018).

**A tertiary hospital in north India.** A 2024 study of 1,000 discharged
patients is the most detailed. Nearly half (49.7%) were covered by Ayushman
Bharat, 36.7% paid cash, 8.8% were under CGHS or ECHS and 4.9% had TPA-managed
insurance. Before any changes:

- the discharge summary reached patients a mean of 235 minutes after the doctor
  wrote the discharge instruction;
- clearance at the Ayushman Bharat counter took a mean of 436 minutes, with long
  queues and document scanning the commonest reasons given;
- CGHS and ECHS clearance took 91 minutes;
- the billing counter took about 26 minutes for cash patients, and returning
  unused medicines about 13 minutes;
- the whole discharge took a mean of 329 minutes.

Among the reasons patients gave for a late summary, about one in six cited no
resident free to write it (17.3%), and as many cited no senior free to
countersign it because they were in theatre, outpatients or casualty (17.7%).
The hospital then attached a checklist to every file at admission, asked heads
of department to begin discharge planning at least a day before the expected
date, opened three dedicated Ayushman Bharat discharge counters in place of one
shared window, and created a discharge team. In a later sample of 200 patients
the mean total fell to 208 minutes, summaries to 73 minutes and Ayushman Bharat
clearance to 163 minutes (Sharma et al., 2025). It was a before-and-after
comparison without a control group, and the spread was wide.

**Is insurance the problem?** A small Pune study found insured patients took far
longer to discharge than uninsured ones (a mean of 572 minutes for 14 insured
patients, against 88 minutes for 61 uninsured), and planned discharges took 85
minutes against 524 for unplanned ones (Mehta et al., 2015). The variation
within each group was very large. A study of 1,000 discharges at a Bengaluru
hospital in January 2014 found something different: patients under a community
health insurance scheme, handled through a sequential, well-defined process, had
significantly shorter turnaround times, and cash patients were not significantly
faster. The authors concluded that a third-party payer does not have to slow a
hospital down (Drew et al., 2016).

The fair reading is that the payer adds steps, but the design of the process
decides how long those steps take.

## Discharge before noon: honest about the evidence

Many hospitals set a target for discharges before noon, on the theory that
morning departures free beds for the day's admissions. The evidence, almost all
from large academic hospitals outside India, is mixed.

- On two medical units at an urban academic hospital, a programme of checklists,
  afternoon multidisciplinary rounds to identify the next day's discharges and
  daily feedback raised discharges before noon from 11% to an average of 38%.
  The average discharge time moved 1 hour 31 minutes earlier, and the ratio of
  observed to expected length of stay fell from 1.06 to 0.96. The fall in 30-day
  readmissions was not statistically significant (Wertheimer et al., 2014).
- In 38,365 hospital stays at another academic centre, 16.9% ended before noon,
  and after adjustment those patients had slightly longer stays, especially
  emergency admissions. The authors could not tell whether patients were kept an
  extra night to achieve a morning discharge (Rajkomar et al., 2016).
- In a randomised trial at three hospitals covering 4,437 patients and 59
  physicians, asking doctors to see their discharges first did not make
  discharge orders or departures meaningfully earlier, and did not change length
  of stay (Burden et al., 2023).
- A 2024 review in the Journal of Hospital Medicine concluded that hospitals
  should stop treating discharge before noon as a goal in itself and focus on
  each patient's readiness and on system-wide flow (Dunn and Lu, 2024).

The useful lesson is not the clock on the wall but the work behind the first
study: planning the next day's discharges the afternoon before. A noon target
that keeps a ready patient in bed overnight is worse than no target.

## Practical steps that do not need a new building

1. **Set an expected date of discharge at admission and tell the family.** NABH
   asks for discharge to be planned with the patient or family. A Cochrane
   review of 33 trials found that a discharge plan tailored to the individual
   probably shortens the initial stay slightly (by about 0.73 days for patients
   with a medical condition) and reduces readmissions (risk ratio 0.89), mainly
   in older patients (Gonçalves-Bradley et al., 2022). Revisit the date on every
   round.
2. **Draft the summary the evening before.** If tomorrow's discharges are known
   by the evening round, a resident can prepare most of the summary overnight
   and the consultant can review it first thing. Give senior countersigning a
   fixed slot, so it does not wait for theatre to end.
3. **Run the steps in parallel, not in a line.** Bill preparation, pharmacy
   returns, the summary and the TPA file barely depend on one another. Start
   them all when the discharge is planned, not when it is ordered.
4. **Return unused medicines before the final bill.** Collect returns from the
   ward the evening before or at the bedside, so the bill is closed once rather
   than reopened after a queue at the pharmacy window.
5. **Open the TPA or scheme file early.** Keep the documents insurers and TPAs
   ask for complete through the stay, so the final request can go out within
   minutes of the bill. Record the exact time the request is sent and the time
   authorisation comes back. Under the IRDAI rule, that timestamp decides who
   bears charges beyond three hours.
6. **Prepare the family for billing.** Tell relatives the day before what to
   bring and how much to expect. The AIIMS Bhubaneswar audit found unprepared
   families were a common cause of billing delay.
7. **Staff the counters to the payer mix.** If half your discharges are under
   Ayushman Bharat, a single shared window is a planned queue. Dedicated
   counters made the largest difference in the north Indian study.
8. **Consider a discharge lounge, and measure it.** A lounge lets a patient who
   is waiting for a summary or transport leave the bed so it can be cleaned. A
   2020 review found only three peer-reviewed reports of lounge implementations,
   wide variation in results, and common problems with under-use and staff
   objections (Franklin et al., 2020). Try one if beds are your binding
   constraint, and check whether bed turnaround actually improves.
9. **Measure each step, not just the total.** Use NABH's definition for the
   overall time, and record a timestamp at every hand-off: discharge order,
   summary signed, pharmacy cleared, bill final, request sent to insurer,
   authorisation received, patient left. Report it monthly by payer and by ward.
   The step that is slowest this month is where the next change should go.

## A closing word

A long discharge is rarely one person's fault. It is a chain of small waits,
each reasonable on its own, that add up to an afternoon. Hospitals that have
shortened it planned earlier, worked in parallel, staffed the bottleneck and
timed every step. That needs no new technology to begin, only someone who owns
the clock from the moment a patient is fit to go home.
