Dengue season is not over. Delhi recorded 98 new cases in the first week of
October, taking its 2026 total to 695, after its highest month of the year
in September (286 cases). West Bengal logged nearly 1,100 cases in the same
week and 10,177 so far this year. Across India, the Health Ministry told
Parliament that 1,13,440 cases and 94 deaths were reported from January to
November 2025, with Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra and Kerala reporting the most.

Most people who catch dengue get better at home. The ones who get into
trouble are often the ones whose families were watching the wrong thing: the
platelet count, rather than the warning signs. This guide explains how the
illness unfolds, what to do at home, which popular remedies to skip, and the
exact signs that mean you should go to hospital.

## How dengue unfolds, day by day

Dengue is a virus spread by *Aedes* mosquitoes, which bite mainly during the
day. According to WHO, symptoms usually start 4 to 10 days after the bite and
last 2 to 7 days. Many infections cause no symptoms at all.

Doctors describe the illness in three phases:

| Phase | Usual timing | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Fever | Usually 2 to 7 days | High fever, severe headache, pain behind the eyes, body and joint pain, nausea, rash |
| Critical | Starts as the fever falls, lasts 24 to 48 hours | In a small number of people, fluid leaks out of the blood vessels. **Watch for the warning signs below.** |
| Recovery | After the critical phase | Leaked fluid is reabsorbed and platelets climb. Tiredness can last weeks |

The most important line in that table is the middle one. The US Centers for
Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) notes that in the critical phase,
leaking plasma can lead to severe disease within a few hours, *even though
most patients seem to be improving*. A family sees the fever break and
relaxes, just as the dangerous day begins. Keep watching for at least two
days after the fever settles.

WHO adds that a previous dengue infection raises the risk of severe dengue
the next time, so someone who has had dengue before should be extra careful.
Most people recover within one to two weeks, though the tiredness can last
several weeks.

## Who needs extra care

India's national dengue guideline, published by the National Center for
Vector Borne Diseases Control (NCVBDC) in 2023, lists the people at higher
risk of severe dengue. They should see a doctor early, and the doctor may
advise admission sooner:

- infants and children under 10, especially if undernourished;
- adults over 65;
- pregnant women, and women who are menstruating or have abnormal vaginal
  bleeding;
- people with obesity;
- people with diabetes, high blood pressure, heart, lung, kidney or liver
  disease;
- people with thalassaemia, G6PD deficiency or other blood disorders, or
  peptic ulcers;
- anyone on long-term steroids or anti-inflammatory painkillers.

## Caring for dengue at home

If a doctor has seen you and says you can be treated at home, the national
guideline and WHO's 2025 clinical guideline agree on the basics.

### Fluids come first

- **Drink often, in small amounts,** especially if you feel sick. WHO says
  water should be the main drink, and oral rehydration solution (ORS) is a
  practical way to replace salts. Coconut water, soups, buttermilk or lassi
  and rice water (kanji) all count.
- **Avoid fizzy soft drinks.** Both guidelines advise against them; WHO
  notes that very sugary drinks can push up blood sugar during the illness.
- **Count the toilet trips.** WHO says enough fluid should mean passing
  urine at least four to six times a day. Fewer than that, or very dark urine,
  means you need more fluid or a doctor's review.
- **Keep a simple log.** WHO suggests measuring what you drink with a cup
  and totalling it daily. A notebook with the time, the drink, the amount,
  each urination and the temperature is enough, and it helps the doctor
  enormously at follow-up.

### Fever and pain: paracetamol only

- **Paracetamol is the medicine to use.** The national guideline advises it
  when the temperature is above 100°F, at 10 mg per kg of body weight per
  dose, no more often than every six hours, and never more than 4 g a day
  for an adult. For children, follow the doctor's dose for their weight.
- **Do not take ibuprofen, aspirin, diclofenac, naproxen or other
  anti-inflammatory painkillers.** WHO makes a *strong* recommendation
  against them in suspected dengue, whatever the severity, because they add
  to the risk of bleeding. Check combination cold and pain tablets for these
  ingredients too.
- **Sponge with lukewarm water** on the forehead, armpits and limbs if the
  fever is high. Do not use ice-cold water.
- **No injections "for quick relief".** The national guideline advises
  against intramuscular injections in dengue because of the bleeding risk.

### Things that do not help

- **Steroids.** WHO advises against corticosteroids for dengue, in both mild
  and severe illness.
- **Antibiotics.** Dengue is a virus. Antibiotics do nothing for it, may
  cause side effects and add to antibiotic resistance. Our guide to
  [antimicrobial resistance](/blog/antimicrobial-resistance-india)
  explains why that matters.
- **Rest is not optional.** Bed rest is part of the national guideline's
  home-care advice. Do not go back to work, school or the gym the day the
  fever breaks.

## The platelet trap

Almost every family with a dengue patient ends up staring at the
platelet count. It falls, everyone panics, and someone starts calling blood
banks. Here is what the evidence actually says.

**The number does not predict bleeding well.** The national guideline
states plainly that platelet counts are unreliable for predicting bleeding.
What makes dengue dangerous is mostly fluid leaking out of the blood vessels,
which a platelet count does not measure. That is why doctors also watch the
haematocrit (how concentrated the blood is), blood pressure, pulse and
urine output.

**Preventive platelet transfusions do not prevent bleeding.** In a
randomised trial published in *The Lancet*, 372 adults with dengue and
platelet counts of 20,000 per microlitre or lower were given either platelet
transfusions plus usual care, or usual care alone. Bleeding occurred in 21%
of the transfusion group and 26% of the comparison group, a difference that
could have been chance. The transfusion group had 13 adverse events against
two, including a severe allergic reaction, a transfusion-related lung injury
and fluid overload. WHO's 2025 guideline now advises against preventive
platelet transfusion in hospitalised patients with counts below 50,000 who
are not bleeding.

Transfusions still have a place. Doctors give blood or platelets when a
patient is actually bleeding heavily or is unstable, and that is a decision
for the treating team. If your doctor says a transfusion is not needed,
that is usually good medicine, not neglect.

**Platelets recover on their own.** CDC describes platelet counts climbing
in the recovery phase, as the body reabsorbs fluid. A low count that is
starting to rise, in someone who is eating, drinking and passing urine, is
usually a good sign.

## Papaya leaf, kiwi and goat milk

Papaya leaf juice is the most popular home remedy for dengue in India. A
2019 systematic review in *BMC Complementary and Alternative Medicine*
looked at nine controlled trials, six of them from India. Most reported that
platelet counts rose a little faster with papaya extract, but the evidence
was rated low quality with a high risk of bias, doses varied, and **no trial
measured what matters**: shock, serious bleeding or death. The authors
concluded that routine use "cannot be recommended".

The practical risk is that bitter raw juice can bring on nausea and
vomiting, which is the last thing a dengue patient needs, and that time
spent chasing a remedy is time not spent drinking fluids or watching for
warning signs. No remedy, including kiwi, dragon fruit, goat milk or
giloy, has been shown to prevent severe dengue. If you want to try
something, tell your doctor first, and never let it replace fluids or delay
a hospital visit.

## Getting the right test at the right time

Fever in October could be dengue, malaria, chikungunya, typhoid or
something else, and the treatment differs. See a doctor for any high fever
rather than guessing.

The national guideline matches the dengue test to the day of illness:

- **NS1 antigen test:** for blood taken on days 1 to 5 of fever.
- **IgM antibody test:** for blood taken after day 5.

So a negative NS1 test on day 6 does not mean you do not have dengue. The
national programme also says rapid card tests vary from batch to batch, and
does not recommend them for confirming dengue or guiding treatment; ELISA
tests in a laboratory are preferred. Your doctor will usually repeat a
complete blood count every day or two during the critical days.

**Dengue or chikungunya?** Both are spread by the same mosquitoes and start
with sudden fever. WHO notes that chikungunya typically causes severe joint
pain that can last weeks, months or even years. Because they are easy to
confuse, WHO advises taking paracetamol, not anti-inflammatory painkillers,
until dengue has been ruled out.

## Red flags: when to go to hospital

**Go to hospital the same day, without waiting for the next blood test, if
the person with dengue has any of these.** They are the warning signs listed
by WHO and the national guideline, and they often appear as the fever
comes down:

- **severe pain in the abdomen,** especially on the upper right side, or
  pain when the belly is touched;
- **vomiting again and again,** or being unable to keep fluids down;
- **bleeding** from the gums or nose, blood in vomit, black tarry stools, or
  new red spots or patches on the skin;
- **passing very little urine,** far less often than usual;
- **fast or difficult breathing;**
- **drowsiness, confusion, restlessness or unusual irritability,** or a
  child who is floppy or will not play;
- **cold, pale or clammy hands and feet;**
- **intense thirst** or extreme weakness.

**Call 112 or go to an emergency department immediately** if the person is
very drowsy or hard to wake, is breathing with great difficulty, has heavy
bleeding, or feels cold and clammy with a weak pulse. These can be signs of
dengue shock, which needs urgent hospital treatment with fluids.

**See a doctor promptly, the same day or next morning,** for any high fever
in a baby, a pregnant woman, an older adult or someone with a long-term
illness. In this season, any high fever deserves a doctor's visit.

Severe dengue is treatable when it is caught early. WHO's 2025 guideline
sets out how hospitals manage it, using careful fluid treatment guided by
simple bedside checks such as how quickly colour returns to a pressed
fingertip. The aim of watching at home is to get the patient there in time.

## Stopping the next bite

The *Aedes* mosquito breeds in clean, still water close to where people
live. It bites mainly in the daytime, so a bed net at night is only part of
the answer. CDC's advice is easy to follow at home:

- **Once a week, empty, scrub, cover or throw out anything holding water:**
  desert coolers, flower-pot saucers, buckets, drums, old tyres, the tray
  under the fridge, coconut shells and plastic on the roof. WHO advises
  covering, emptying and cleaning household water containers every week.
- **Cover water tanks and storage drums** with tight lids.
- **Use a repellent** with DEET, picaridin (icaridin), IR3535, oil of lemon
  eucalyptus or PMD, following the label. Do not use oil of lemon eucalyptus
  or PMD on children under 3. Adults should put repellent on their own hands
  first and then on a child's face, avoiding the eyes, mouth and hands. If
  you use sunscreen, put it on first.
- **Cover up** with loose, long sleeves and full-length trousers, and use
  mosquito netting over cots, strollers and baby carriers.
- **Fit screens** on windows and doors where you can.
- **Protect the patient too.** WHO says a person with dengue can pass the
  virus to mosquitoes from about two days before symptoms start until about
  two days after the fever ends, so keep them under a net and use repellent.

## What about a vaccine?

There is news here. The drugs regulator CDSCO approved Takeda's dengue vaccine,
Qdenga, in July 2026, for people aged 4 to 60. It is given as two injections
three months apart, and the company must run a safety and effectiveness
study in India after launch. At the time of approval, its launch date, price
and any use in government programmes had not been announced. WHO currently
recommends Qdenga for children aged 6 to 16 in places with high transmission.

DengiAll, a vaccine developed by Panacea Biotec with ICMR, began a
phase 3 trial in August 2024 with more than 10,335 adults at 19 sites in 18
states and union territories. Ask your doctor whether a vaccine makes sense
for your family once it is available. In the meantime, no vaccine changes
the advice for someone who has dengue now.

## The bottom line

Most dengue is mild and gets better with rest, fluids and paracetamol.
Watch the person, not just the platelet count: the danger window is the day
or two after the fever falls. Keep a simple log of drinks, urine and
temperature, avoid anti-inflammatory painkillers, injections and steroids,
and go to hospital straight away for belly pain, repeated vomiting,
bleeding, drowsiness, fast breathing or cold, clammy skin. Then empty the
cooler and the flower-pot saucers, so nobody else in the house is next.

*This article is general health information, not medical advice. If you or
a family member has a fever, see a doctor. In an emergency, call 112 or go
to the nearest hospital.*
