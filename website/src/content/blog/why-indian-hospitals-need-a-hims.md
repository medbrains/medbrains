*The scene below is illustrative. It is a composite, not a real patient or hospital.*

It is 9.40 on a Monday morning at a 120-bed hospital in a district town. The
registration counter has a queue out of the door, and each patient is giving
their name, age, address and phone number again, because last year's file is
in a cupboard nobody can find. Upstairs, a physician orders a creatinine that
was done two days ago — the report is on paper in another ward. A discharge
planned for 11 is still waiting at 4, because the bill has to be built by
hand from charge slips, and two of them are missing. Nobody in this story is
careless. The system they work in is made of paper, and paper forgets.

A **hospital information and management system (HIMS)** is the software that
replaces that paper: registration, OPD, wards, orders, pharmacy, lab,
radiology, billing and records, on one patient identity. This article is about
what a HIMS actually changes, why India needs it now, and why the same system
is the foundation for research that Indian medicine badly needs.

## What a HIMS actually changes

The case for hospital information systems is not new. The Institute of
Medicine's [*To Err Is Human* (2000)](https://www.nationalacademies.org/publications/9728)
reported expert estimates that as many as 98,000 people a year died from
medical errors in US hospitals, and concluded that the problem is mostly good
people working in bad systems. Its follow-up, [*Crossing the Quality Chasm* (2001)](https://www.nationalacademies.org/publications/10027),
set six aims for care — safe, effective, patient-centred, timely, efficient
and equitable — and named information technology as one of the levers to get
there. The evidence since then is specific.

### Fewer medication errors

In a landmark study at a Boston teaching hospital, computerised physician order
entry (CPOE) with dose, allergy and interaction checks cut non-intercepted
serious medication errors by 55%
([Bates et al., JAMA, 1998](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.280.15.1311)). A later
meta-analysis estimated that an order placed through CPOE is 48% less likely to
contain an error than a paper order
([Radley et al., JAMIA, 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3628057/)).
The same authors note that fewer errors do not automatically mean less harm,
and well-designed checks matter more than the screen itself.

### Fewer repeat tests

When the last result is visible at the moment of ordering, clinicians repeat
less. In one emergency department, a simple visual cue in the electronic
ordering screen was followed by a 49% fall in unintentional duplicate lab orders
([Horng et al., JAMA Network Open, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6902748/)).
For patients paying out of pocket, every avoided repeat test is money saved.

### Shorter queues

Indian evidence is growing. At a teaching hospital in Agra, patients who
registered online with ABHA took an average of 4.15 minutes, against 10.37
minutes at the conventional counter — a small, single-site study, but a clear
direction ([Ranjan et al., Cureus, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12063933/)).
The government reports that ABHA-based Scan and Share registration has issued
over 23.21 crore queue tokens as of 18 June 2026
([PIB, July 2026](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2026/jul/doc202676912801.pdf)).

### What else changes, day to day

Not every benefit has a randomised trial behind it, but administrators will
recognise these:

- **Patient identification.** One identity per patient, with two identifiers
  on every wristband, label and report — the first of the international patient
  safety goals that NABH and JCI audit against.
- **Billing integrity.** Charges are captured where the service happens — the
  lab, the pharmacy, the OT — instead of being reconstructed from slips at
  discharge. Leakage and disputed bills both fall.
- **Bed management.** A live view of which beds are occupied, being cleaned or
  free, instead of a phone round of the wards.
- **Audit readiness.** When every order, administration and signature carries
  a time and a name, an NABH assessment becomes a report you run, not a month
  of file-pulling.
- **Turnaround times.** Order-to-result and admission-to-discharge times can
  only be improved once they are measured.

## Why India needs this now

### The workload is enormous, and mostly private

India has 13,88,185 registered allopathic doctors and 7,51,768 registered AYUSH
practitioners; the government's estimate of a 1:811 doctor-population ratio
assumes 80% of both groups are available
([PIB, February 2026](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2225755&reg=3&lang=1)).
Those doctors see very large volumes, and much of that care happens outside
government hospitals: in the NSS 75th round (2017-18), public hospitals
treated only 46% of hospitalised cases in rural areas and 35% in urban areas
([MoSPI, 2019](https://mospi.gov.in/sites/default/files/announcements/Summary%20Analysis_Report_586_Health.pdf)).
The private and trust hospitals that carry the rest are where digital
systems will make the most difference — and where adoption is most uneven.

### The national digital rails now exist

The Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM) has built the public infrastructure
a HIMS plugs into:

- **ABHA**, the patient's health ID. Over 104 crore health records are linked
  to over 93 crore ABHA accounts
  ([PIB, July 2026](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2026/jul/doc202676912801.pdf)).
- **The Health Facility Registry and Healthcare Professionals Registry.** As of
  12 December 2025, about 4.42 lakh facilities and 7.47 lakh professionals were
  registered ([MoHFW, December 2025](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2026/jan/doc202611749801.pdf)).
- **The National Health Claims Exchange (NHCX)**, a common digital channel
  for insurance claims between hospitals, insurers and payers
  ([PIB, July 2026](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2026/jul/doc202676912801.pdf)).
- **The Digital Health Incentive Scheme (DHIS).** Under the November 2025
  terms, hospitals earned Rs 20 per eligible health record linked to a
  KYC-verified ABHA, above 100 such records a month, for November 2025 to
  March 2026 ([NHA, 2025](https://abdm.gov.in/strapicms/uploads/20_Nov_2025_vf_DHIS_Corrigendum_6_dba5b58a53.pdf)).
  By 18 June 2026, hospitals had been paid over Rs 107 crore
  ([PIB, July 2026](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2026/jul/doc202676912801.pdf)).
  Check the ABDM site for the terms in force today.

Software integration with ABDM is usually described in milestones: **M1**
(creating and verifying ABHA), **M2** (acting as a health information
provider, linking records so patients can share them with consent) and **M3**
(acting as a health information user, fetching records a patient has
consented to share). A hospital without a HIMS that does these steps cannot
take part in any of it.

### Insurance, PM-JAY and the claim trail

As of 1 December 2025, PM-JAY had issued about 42.48 crore Ayushman cards and
authorised 10.98 crore hospital admissions worth Rs 1.60 lakh crore
([MoHFW, December 2025](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2026/jan/doc202611749801.pdf)).
Every one of those admissions needs pre-authorisation, documents and a clean
claim. A hospital that builds its claim from structured clinical and billing
data gets paid faster and argues less.

### The law now expects it

The [Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/06/2bf1f0e9f04e6fb4f8fef35e82c42aa5.pdf)
applies to every hospital that holds patient data. Its Rules were notified on
14 November 2025 with an eighteen-month phased compliance period, and the
highest penalty — up to Rs 250 crore — applies to failing to keep reasonable
security safeguards
([PIB, November 2025](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2025/nov/doc20251117695301.pdf)).
Consent notices, breach reporting and access controls are very hard to prove
on paper.

Accreditation is moving the same way. In September 2024 NABH published its
first Digital Health Standards for HIS/EMR systems, and a product needs a valid
ABDM M3 compliance certificate and at least three live hospital deployments to
apply ([NABH, 2024](https://nabh.co/programmes/digital-health-standards-his-emr-systems/)).

## Why research in India — and why it needs efficient tools

India's research output does not match the size of its population or its
illness. India has nearly 20% of the world's population but hosts only about
1.5% of global clinical trials
([Cherian et al., Communications Medicine, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12216851/)).
Of trials registered in India's CTRI from 2007 to 2014, only 18% studied the
ten conditions causing 68.3% of deaths
([Kaur et al., 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7574753/)).
An analysis of 579 Indian medical institutions found that 332 medical colleges
(57.3%) published no research paper between 2005 and 2014
([as summarised in J Postgrad Med, 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5105204/)).

The reasons are many — time, funding, training, incentives. But one reason is
practical and fixable: **the data from everyday care is not usable.** Every
Indian hospital sees thousands of patients a month with diabetes, TB,
hypertension, dengue, trauma and high-risk pregnancy. That is real-world data
of great value. On paper, or in free-text fields, it is almost impossible to
count, compare or follow over time. A postgraduate student writing a thesis —
required of MD and MS students under the NMC's PGMER-2023
([Careers360, 2024](https://news.careers360.com/nmc-clarifies-thesis-requirement-students-can-appear-for-final-exams-without-hods-approval-on-dissertation))
— can spend months pulling files by hand to collect a few hundred cases.

### What "research-ready" care data means

Data from routine care becomes usable for research when it is:

1. **Structured.** A blood pressure is two numbers in fields, not a phrase in a
   note.
2. **Coded.** Diagnoses in ICD-10 or ICD-11, lab tests in LOINC, clinical
   findings in SNOMED CT — so "MI", "heart attack" and "STEMI" count as the
   same thing across departments and hospitals.
3. **Consented.** Patients know how their data may be used, and consent is
   recorded rather than assumed, in line with the DPDP Act.
4. **De-identifiable.** Names, phone numbers and ABHA numbers can be removed or
   replaced before data leaves the clinical team.
5. **Auditable.** Every access to the data is logged, so an ethics committee
   can see who looked at what.

This matters for ethics approval. ICMR's national guidelines allow an ethics
committee to waive consent for retrospective studies where participants are
de-identified or cannot be contacted, provided the research carries less than
minimal risk ([ICMR, 2017, section 5.7](https://ethics.ncdirindia.org/asset/pdf/ICMR_National_Ethical_Guidelines.pdf)).
A system that can de-identify cleanly and prove who accessed what makes that
case far easier to make — and far safer for patients.

## Why research and hospital management belong together

It is tempting to treat research as something a medical college does, separate
from running the hospital. In practice the same system produces both. The
order a doctor places to treat a patient is the same record a researcher later
counts. If the ward system is poor, the research is poor.

The Institute of Medicine called the goal a **learning health system**: one in
which the information generated by care is captured and fed back to improve
care ([*Best Care at Lower Cost*, 2013](https://www.nationalacademies.org/publications/13444)).
For an Indian hospital, that loop looks ordinary:

- The infection-control nurse sees surgical-site infection rates by surgeon
  and procedure every month, not once a year.
- The pharmacy committee sees antibiotic use by ward and changes the formulary.
- A department runs a registry — for stroke, snakebite, or high-risk
  pregnancy — straight from the records it already keeps.
- A teaching hospital gives every PG student a de-identified, coded dataset to
  start from, with ethics approval built into the request.

Management gets better numbers, clinicians get better feedback, and research
becomes a by-product of good care instead of a separate project.

## What to look for in a HIMS

A vendor-neutral checklist for hospital owners, CEOs and medical directors:

- **ABDM integration.** Which milestones (M1, M2, M3) are certified today, and
  which are in progress? Ask for the certificate, not the brochure. Ask about
  NHCX for claims.
- **NABH support.** Does it capture what assessors check — two-identifier
  patient ID, medication reconciliation, consent, high-alert drugs, falls,
  infection control, incident reporting?
- **Coding standards.** ICD for diagnoses, LOINC for labs, SNOMED CT for
  clinical terms, and FHIR for exchange.
- **Your data, your exit.** Can you export everything, in open formats, without
  paying the vendor? Who owns the data in the contract?
- **Access control and audit trail.** Role-based access, with every view and
  change of a patient record logged — essential under the DPDP Act.
- **Downtime and connectivity.** What happens when the internet fails in a
  district town? Can wards keep working and sync later?
- **Languages.** Can staff and patients use it in the languages they speak?
- **Research features.** De-identified export, registries, cohort queries,
  consent records — especially if you are, or plan to become, a medical college.
- **Total cost.** Licence, implementation, hardware, training, support and
  upgrades over five years, not just the first-year price.
