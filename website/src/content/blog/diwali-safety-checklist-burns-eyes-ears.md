Diwali falls on Sunday 8 November this year. For most families it is the
best night of the year. For emergency, burns and eye departments it is one of
the busiest. Last Diwali, AIIMS New Delhi's eye centre treated 190 people with
firecracker and carbide gun injuries over the festival, 19% more than the year
before, and about 60 children needed eye surgery. In Madhya Pradesh, nearly
300 people, many of them children, were hurt by home-made carbide "guns".

Almost all of it is preventable, and the first few minutes after an injury
change how it heals. This checklist covers what injures people, the first aid
that helps, the remedies that make things worse, and when to go to hospital.

## What injuries does Diwali actually cause?

Indian hospital studies tell a consistent story.

- **Burns, mostly small but on the hands and face.** Safdarjung Hospital in
  Delhi saw 1,373 firecracker injuries over the Diwali days of 2002 to 2010.
  73% of patients were aged 5 to 30, and 91% had burns covering less than 5%
  of the body. Small does not mean minor: a burn on the palm or fingers can
  scar and stiffen a hand for life.
- **Diyas, not just crackers.** In the same hospital's 2018 to 2022 records,
  32.5% of Diwali burns were caused by diyas, most often a lit diya left on the
  floor. 70.4% of these patients were women, and one in three needed
  admission. A saree, dupatta or lehenga that brushes a floor diya catches
  fast.
- **Children who were alone.** In a Delhi study of 65 children hurt on Diwali
  night, 90% were unsupervised. The hands (62%), face (32%) and eyes (10%) were
  injured most, and the commonest single mistake was lighting a cracker while
  holding it.
- **Eyes, including bystanders.** A 2023 to 2024 study across three eye
  hospitals in Central and Western India found 62.7% of the 67 patients were
  children and 34.3% were bystanders, not the person lighting the cracker.
  More than 40% arrived seeing worse than 6/60, and the average cost of care
  was ₹31,500. At CMC Vellore, one in every 12 children with a firework eye
  injury lost the sight of that eye.

## How do you give first aid for a burn?

This is the advice from WHO and the NHS. Learn it before the night, not
during it.

1. **Stop the burning.** Move the person away from the fire. If clothes are
   alight, stop them running, get them to the ground and roll them, or smother
   the flames with a blanket.
2. **Cool the burn under cool running tap water for 20 minutes.** Start as
   soon as you can. If there is no tap, pour clean cool water from a bottle or
   bucket, or use a wet cloth. Twenty minutes feels long; set a timer.
3. **Do not use ice, iced water, butter, oil, ghee, toothpaste, haldi or any
   paste.** WHO lists all of these as things not to put on a burn.
4. **Take off rings, bangles, watches and clothing near the burn** before it
   swells, but leave anything that is stuck to the skin.
5. **Keep the person warm.** Cool the burn, not the whole person. This
   matters most for children and large burns.
6. **Cover the burn loosely** with cling film laid over it (not wrapped round
   a limb) or a clean, non-fluffy cloth. No cotton wool, no sticky plasters.
7. **Do not burst blisters.** For pain, paracetamol as the label directs is
   reasonable.
8. **Get help** for any burn on the face, hands, feet or genitals, any large
   or deep burn, any chemical or electrical burn, or any burn in a baby or an
   older adult. Call 112 or 108 for a serious burn.

## What should you do for a firecracker eye injury?

Eye injuries are where good intentions do the most harm. There are two
situations, and they need opposite things.

**If the eye was hit by a blast or a fragment, is bleeding or cut, looks
misshapen, or has something stuck in it:**

1. **Do not rub it.** The American Academy of Ophthalmology warns that
   rubbing can make the injury worse.
2. **Do not rinse it, press on it, or put on any drops, ointment, ghee or
   rose water.**
3. **Do not try to pull anything out**, however small it looks. The NHS says
   never remove an object that has pierced the eye.
4. **Shield it without pressure.** Tape the bottom half of a paper cup over
   the eye, resting on the bones around it, so nothing touches the eyeball.
5. **Go to an eye hospital or emergency department at once**, and do not let
   the injured person drive. The AAO also advises against taking pain
   medicines before seeing a doctor.

**If it was only powder, smoke or a chemical splash, with no cut or wound:**

1. **Rinse with plenty of clean running water for at least 20 minutes**,
   holding the eyelids open, as the NHS advises for chemicals.
2. **Then go to an eye hospital the same night**, and keep rinsing on the way
   if you can. Chemical burns can look mild at first.

## Why are carbide guns so dangerous?

Carbide "guns" are home-made pipes filled with calcium carbide, which gives
off an explosive gas when it meets water. They spread through social media
videos last year and are sold as toys.

At IGIMS Patna, 37 patients came in with carbide gun eye injuries in just 10
days of October 2025. All were male, with a median age of 15. Every injury
happened at home, mostly while the boy was **looking into the pipe after it
failed to fire**. Almost two thirds had severe chemical and heat burns of the
eye, which can damage the stem cells the cornea needs to heal. Bhopal, Indore
and Gwalior banned the devices after last year's injuries, and AIIMS Delhi
alone treated 18 to 20 carbide gun injuries.

The rule is simple: do not buy, make or allow carbide guns, and if any device
or cracker fails to go off, never look into it or go back to it.

## How loud is too loud for your ears?

Indian law already caps cracker noise. Under the Environment (Protection)
Rules, as published by the Central Pollution Control Board, crackers louder
than **125 dB(AI) or 145 dB(C)pk, measured 4 metres from where they burst**,
cannot be made, sold or used, and the limit is lower for joined "series"
crackers. Since 2005 the Supreme Court has barred sound-emitting crackers
between **10 pm and 6 am**. In 2026 the Court asked the CPCB whether some
cracker types could be allowed to be louder, but no such order has been
passed, so the existing limits stand.

WHO's community noise guidelines say impulse sounds such as fireworks should
never exceed a peak of **140 dB for adults and 120 dB for children**,
measured close to the ear. A study of firecracker injuries found most
crackers were louder than 110 dB even at 6 metres, and that blast noise
damages both hearing and balance organs in the inner ear.

To protect ears:

- keep children, babies and older relatives well back, and indoors for the
  biggest bombs and series crackers;
- use foam earplugs or ear muffs for anyone who must be close, especially
  children;
- never light a cracker in a narrow lane or stairwell, where the sound
  bounces back.

Ringing or muffled hearing after a loud bang often settles within a day. The
NHS advises an urgent doctor's appointment for sudden hearing loss in one or
both ears, or hearing loss with ear pain or discharge, because some causes
need prompt treatment. Bleeding from the ear, severe pain or dizziness after a
blast needs an emergency department.

## Do green crackers protect your lungs?

Only partly. Green crackers are lower-emission products developed under
CSIR-NEERI and must carry a QR code you can scan to check them. They are less
polluting, not pollution-free, and they still burn and bang like any other
cracker.

The morning after Diwali often has the worst air of the year. Anyone with
asthma, COPD or heart disease should stay indoors with windows shut while
crackers are going off nearby, keep taking their regular medicines, and carry
their reliever inhaler. Our [guide to the air pollution
season](/blog/air-pollution-season-health-guide) covers masks, AQI and when
to see a doctor.

## How do you protect children, older relatives and pets?

**Children**

- An adult lights every cracker. Children watch from a marked line.
- Very young children should not hold even sparklers; older children hold
  them at arm's length, one at a time, and drop finished ones into water.
- Cotton clothes that fit, not synthetic, flowing or layered outfits, as the
  Delhi children's study recommends.
- No crackers in hands, pockets or under tins, and no "throwing" crackers.

**Older relatives**

- Place diyas on raised, stable surfaces, away from curtains and the edge of
  saree pallus and dupattas. The Safdarjung diya study points straight at
  floor diyas.
- Keep walkways clear and lit, so nobody trips over a diya or a spent box.
- Bring people with lung or heart disease indoors before the bursting starts,
  with their medicines to hand.

**Pets and street animals**

The RSPCA's advice translates well to Indian homes: walk dogs in daylight,
make a quiet "safe den" indoors before dark, close windows and curtains, let a
frightened animal hide rather than coaxing it out, and make sure it wears a
collar with your phone number in case it bolts. Ask your vet in advance if
your pet panics every year. Never burst crackers near animals, cattle or
shelters.

## The Diwali safety checklist

Screenshot this, or print it for the gate.

**Before the night**

- Buy only from licensed shops; check the QR code on green crackers
- No carbide guns, no home-made crackers, no "bombs" from social media
- Two buckets of water, a bucket of sand and a cotton blanket by the
  bursting spot
- Earplugs or ear muffs for children
- Save 112 and 108, and know your nearest emergency and eye hospitals
- Diyas on raised, stable surfaces, away from curtains
- Inhalers and regular medicines in reach for anyone with asthma or COPD

**During**

- An adult lights every cracker, with a long incense stick, at arm's length
- Open ground, away from vehicles, dry leaves and buildings
- Cotton clothes; tie back hair, dupattas and saree pallus
- Everyone else stays behind a line several metres back
- One cracker at a time; never relight a dud, never look into it
- Spent sparklers and duds straight into the water bucket
- Stop by 10 pm, or earlier if your state's order says so

**After**

- Soak all spent crackers in water before throwing them out
- Count heads, check hands and eyes, and ask about ringing ears
- Windows shut the next morning if the air is bad

## Myths and facts about Diwali injuries

**1. Myth: "Ice is the fastest way to cool a burn."**

Fact: WHO says not to put ice on a burn. Cool running tap water for about 20
minutes is what helps, and the body should be kept warm.

**2. Myth: "Toothpaste, butter or haldi soothes a burn."**

Fact: They may feel cool for a moment, but WHO lists toothpaste, butter, oils
and traditional pastes among things never to put on a burn. They do not cool
the skin, and the doctor then has to clean them off a raw wound.

**3. Myth: "Pop the blister so it heals faster."**

Fact: A blister is a natural dressing. Both WHO and the NHS say do not burst
it.

**4. Myth: "Rub the eye to get the speck out."**

Fact: Rubbing can turn a small scratch into a deeper injury, or push a
fragment further in. Do not rub, press or dig.

**5. Partly true: "Wash the eye with water straight away."**

Fact: Right for powder, smoke or a chemical splash: rinse for at least 20
minutes. Wrong if the eye was hit by a blast, is cut or bleeding, or has
something stuck in it: then do not rinse; shield it and go.

**6. Myth: "Green crackers are safe."**

Fact: They pollute less, but they are not pollution-free, and they burn and
bang like any cracker. All the burn, eye and noise precautions still apply.

**7. Myth: "If a cracker doesn't go off, just check it."**

Fact: Duds can go off late. In the Patna carbide gun study, every injury
happened at home, mostly while someone looked into a device that had failed
to fire. Leave it, wait, then soak it in water from a distance.

## Red flags: when to call 112 or 108

**Call 112 (or 108 for an ambulance) or go to an emergency department at
once if:**

- a burn is large, deep, charred, white or leathery, or on the face, hands,
  feet, genitals or a joint;
- someone breathed in smoke and is coughing, hoarse, wheezing or has soot
  around the nose or mouth;
- clothes caught fire on a person, or the injured person is a baby or an
  older adult;
- there was a blast injury to the eye, any change in vision, bleeding from
  the eye, or something stuck in it;
- a chemical or carbide splash reached the eye, even if it now feels better;
- there is bleeding from the ear, severe ear pain or dizziness after a blast;
- a hand was injured by a cracker that burst while held.

**See a doctor within a day** if a burn blisters widely or becomes more red,
swollen or painful, if hearing has not returned to normal, or if an eye stays
red, watery or sensitive to light.

## The bottom line

Most Diwali injuries come from a few predictable moments: a cracker lit in the
hand, a dud someone went back to, a child left alone, a diya on the floor, a
face too close to a bang. An adult in charge, a bucket of water and a few
metres of distance prevent most of them. If someone is hurt, cool a burn under
running water for 20 minutes, never ice or toothpaste; never rub, press or
rinse an eye that has been hit, and shield it on the way to hospital; and do
not let anyone near a carbide gun.

*This article is general health information, not medical advice. In an
emergency, call 112 or 108, or go to the nearest hospital.*
