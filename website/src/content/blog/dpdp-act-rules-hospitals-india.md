Every hospital runs on personal data: a name and phone number at registration,
a diagnosis in the OPD, results from the laboratory, a policy number at
billing. Most of it now lives in software. The **Digital Personal Data
Protection Act, 2023** (the DPDP Act) and the **Digital Personal Data
Protection Rules, 2025** set out how that data may be collected, used, kept
and protected.

The Rules were published in the Gazette dated 13 November 2025, after a
consultation that drew 6,915 inputs (PIB, 2025). The main duties start in
2027. This post explains what the law asks of a hospital, where it meets
existing medical-records duties, and what to do first.

*This is general information, not legal advice. Take advice on your own
hospital's position.*

## The timeline

The Act and the Rules come into force in three stages, counted from 13
November 2025:

- **Already in force:** definitions and the provisions setting up the Data
  Protection Board and its procedures.
- **After one year (November 2026):** registration and duties of Consent
  Managers (Rule 4, section 6(9)).
- **After eighteen months (May 2027):** everything operational: notice and
  consent, legitimate uses, security, breach reporting, retention, children's
  data, patients' rights and Significant Data Fiduciaries (Rules 3, 5 to 16,
  22 and 23; sections 3 to 17).

Lawyers count these dates slightly differently: AZB & Partners gives 12
November 2026 and 12 May 2027, others the 13th. Plan to the earlier date. In
January 2026 MeitY also consulted on cutting the eighteen months to twelve, at
least for some organisations (S.S. Rana & Co., 2026). A September 2026 review
found no amending notification (MirvoLegal, 2026), but it is worth watching.

## Is a hospital a Data Fiduciary?

Yes. A **Data Fiduciary** is any person who decides the purpose and means of
processing personal data (section 2(i)), and "person" includes companies,
firms, associations and the State. Private and government hospitals, nursing
homes and single-doctor clinics are all covered. The patient is the **Data
Principal**; for a child, that includes the parent or lawful guardian (section
2(j)).

A cloud host or transcription service working on the hospital's behalf is a
**Data Processor**. The hospital remains responsible for what processors do,
may use them only under a valid contract, and must make them erase data when
it erases its own (section 8).

The Act covers data collected digitally and paper data that is later digitised
(section 3), which in practice reaches most patient records. Health data is
not a separate category, but its sensitivity counts: the Board must weigh the
type of data affected when setting a penalty (section 33(2)).

## Consent and notice

A hospital may process personal data only with **consent** or for a
**legitimate use** listed in section 7 (section 4).

Consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, and
limited to the data needed for the stated purpose (section 6(1)). It must come
with a **notice** that, under Rule 3:

- stands on its own, separate from other information;
- gives an **itemised description** of the data and the specific purposes, in
  plain language;
- explains how to withdraw consent, exercise rights and complain to the Board.

Patients must be able to read the notice and consent request in English or
**any language in the Eighth Schedule** to the Constitution (sections 5(3),
6(3)). Withdrawing consent must be as easy as giving it, after which
processing must stop within a reasonable time unless a law requires otherwise
(sections 6(4), 6(6)). In any dispute, the hospital must prove notice and
consent were given (section 6(10)), so consent records become evidence.
Patients who consented before the Act need a fresh notice as soon as
reasonably practicable (section 5(2)).

## The legitimate-use exceptions

Section 7 lists situations where consent is not needed. Three matter most to
hospitals:

- **Medical emergencies.** Section 7(f) covers a medical emergency involving
  "a threat to the life or immediate threat to the health" of the patient or
  anyone else. It does not cover routine or planned care.
- **Public health.** Section 7(g) covers treatment and health services during
  an epidemic, outbreak or other threat to public health.
- **Legal duties.** Section 7(d) covers disclosures a law requires you to make
  to the State; section 7(e) covers court orders.

Section 7(a) also allows use of data a person voluntarily provided for a
purpose, unless she objects. The Act's example is a pharmacy texting a
receipt. How far that stretches to routine treatment is not settled. Our
reading is to rely on it only for what the patient plainly came for.

The exceptions are narrow in time too. King Stubb & Kasiva, a law firm, notes
that emergency processing must stay within the emergency; using that data
later for marketing needs consent.

## Children and guardians

A child is anyone under eighteen (section 2(f)). Normally a fiduciary needs
**verifiable parental consent** before processing a child's data, and may not
track or behaviourally monitor children (sections 9(1), 9(3)). Rule 10
explains how a parent's identity and age are checked.

The Rules exempt healthcare. Part A of the Fourth Schedule lifts sections 9(1)
and 9(3) for **clinical establishments, mental health establishments and
healthcare professionals**, but only for providing health services to the
child, to the extent necessary to protect her health. Allied healthcare
professionals have a similar exemption for supporting a treatment and referral
plan. Marketing and research are not covered, and section 9(2), no processing
likely to harm a child's well-being, still applies.

For adults with a disability, Rule 11 requires the hospital to verify that a
guardian was appointed by a court, designated authority or local level
committee.

## Security safeguards

Rule 6 sets minimum safeguards:

- encryption, masking or tokenisation of personal data;
- access control on systems used by the hospital and its processors;
- logs, monitoring and review to detect and investigate unauthorised access;
- backups, so care can continue if data is lost or locked;
- logs and related data kept for **one year**, unless another law says
  otherwise;
- security clauses in processor contracts.

Rule 8(3) separately requires personal data, traffic data and processing logs
to be kept for **at least one year** from processing.

## Breach notification

A breach includes unauthorised processing, accidental disclosure or loss of
access that compromises confidentiality, integrity or availability (section
2(u)). Ransomware that locks the system counts, even if nothing leaks.

Under Rule 7, the hospital must tell **affected patients** without delay, in
plain language: what happened, likely consequences, what is being done, what
they can do, and a contact person. It must tell **the Board** without delay,
then send a detailed report within **72 hours** of becoming aware, covering
causes, mitigation, who was responsible, prevention and the notices sent to
patients. The Board may allow longer on written request. There is no minimum
size for a reportable breach.

## Retention and medical records

The Act asks for erasure once consent is withdrawn or the purpose is served,
**unless retention is needed to comply with a law** (section 8(7)); erasure
requests carry the same exception (section 12(3)). The fixed deletion periods
in the Third Schedule apply only to large e-commerce, gaming and social media
platforms.

Older medical-records duties still apply:

- The Indian Medical Council ethics regulations of 2002 require **indoor
  patients' records to be kept for three years** from the start of treatment,
  with copies supplied within 72 hours of a request.
- Where the Clinical Establishments (Central Government) Rules, 2012 apply,
  establishments must maintain electronic medical or health records of every
  patient as specified by government (rule 9(iv)).

The Act applies in addition to other laws (section 38(1)), and its erasure
duties give way where a law requires retention. A patient cannot make a
hospital destroy records it must legally keep. But retention needs a reason. A
written schedule linking each record type to its legal basis is the practical
answer.

## Significant Data Fiduciaries

The government may notify a fiduciary, or a class of them, as **Significant**,
considering factors such as the volume and sensitivity of data (section 10).
Large hospital chains and diagnostic networks should not rule this out. These
must appoint a Data Protection Officer based in India and an independent
auditor, run an impact assessment and audit **every twelve months**, check
their algorithms, and may be required to keep specified data in India (Rule
13).

## Penalties

The Board inquires into breaches and can impose penalties up to:

- **₹250 crore** for failing to take reasonable security safeguards;
- **₹200 crore** for failing to notify a breach;
- **₹200 crore** for breaching duties on children's data;
- **₹150 crore** for breaching Significant Data Fiduciary duties;
- **₹50 crore** for any other breach.

These are ceilings. The Board must weigh gravity, duration, the data involved,
repetition, and how quickly the hospital acted to limit harm (section 33(2)).
Patients must use the hospital's grievance process before going to the Board
(section 13(3)), and appeals go to the Telecom Disputes Settlement and
Appellate Tribunal.

## Consent Managers and ABDM

A **Consent Manager** is a platform registered with the Board through which
people give, manage and withdraw consent (section 2(g)). It must be an Indian
company with a net worth of at least ₹2 crore, must not be able to read the
data it routes, and must keep consent records for seven years (First
Schedule).

Under the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission, the Health Information Exchange and
Consent Manager (HIE-CM) already moves records between providers only after
the patient's consent (PIB, 2025). That consent can cover selected records
rather than everything (PIB, 2024), and records stay with the provider that
created them (SFLC.in, 2024). In our reading, ABDM consent governs sharing
through the ABDM network. It does not replace the hospital's own DPDP notice
and lawful basis for billing, reminders, claims or analytics.

## A checklist for the months ahead

1. **Name an owner** for data protection and publish their contact on your
   website and app (Rule 9).
2. **Map your data:** what each department collects, why, where it is stored,
   who can see it and who receives it.
3. **Set a lawful basis** for each purpose: consent or a section 7 use. Be
   strict about emergencies.
4. **Rewrite registration notices:** itemised, plain, standalone, in English
   and the Eighth Schedule languages your patients use. Plan notices for
   existing patients.
5. **Keep consent records:** which notice version, what was agreed, when, and
   any withdrawal.
6. **Tighten access and logs:** role-based access, encryption, and access logs
   reviewed and kept for a year.
7. **Run a breach drill** against the 72-hour clock, with templates for the
   Board and patients.
8. **Review contracts** with cloud vendors, outsourced labs, TPAs and
   insurers: decide who is a processor and who is a separate fiduciary, and
   add security, breach and erasure clauses.
9. **Write a retention schedule** grounded in the laws that apply to you, with
   secure erasure.
10. **Set up rights and grievances:** access, correction, erasure and
    nomination, with a published response period of no more than ninety days
    (Rule 14).
11. **Check children's processing** against the Fourth Schedule exemption.
12. **Train staff** at the front desk, wards, MRD, billing and IT.

## A closing word

Most of what the Act asks is what good medical-records practice already
expects: collect what you need, say why, protect it, keep it as long as the
law requires, and report quickly when something goes wrong. The difference is
that it will now be enforceable. A data map and a new registration notice this
year leave time to do the rest calmly.
