Every winter, the same news story runs with different names.
In January 2024, Delhi's fire service said 15 people had died in the city
that month after sleeping in closed rooms with an angithi, a coal brazier,
and that the toll passed 20 once the rest of the National Capital Region was
counted. In the first week of January 2025, Jammu and Kashmir reported 11
deaths from suffocation in a single week, including a family of five in one
rented room in Srinagar.

These are not freak accidents. They are carbon monoxide poisoning, and it is
almost entirely preventable. Yet most of what you will find about it online
is either a news report after a death or advice written for American homes
with central heating. This guide is for homes heated by fire and gas: the angithi and the
sigri, the bukhari and the kangri, the LPG geyser in a small bathroom, the
car parked with its engine running for warmth. It explains what the gas
does, who is most at risk, the early signs, what to do in the first minutes,
and the habits that keep a family safe.

## What is carbon monoxide, and why can't you sense it?

Carbon monoxide (CO) is a gas made whenever a fuel burns without enough
oxygen: coal, charcoal, wood, cow-dung cakes, kerosene, LPG, piped gas,
petrol or diesel. The National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA)
describes it as colourless, odourless and tasteless. That is the whole
danger. Smoke can make your eyes water, but the CO that comes with it gives
no warning of its own, and a bed of glowing coals with no visible smoke can
still be giving it off.

Once breathed in, CO takes the place of oxygen in the blood, so the brain
and heart are starved of oxygen even though the person is breathing
normally. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) warns that
people who are asleep, or who have been drinking, can die before they ever
notice a symptom. That is why so many cases are found in the
morning.

Worldwide, a study from the Global Burden of Disease project estimated that
unintentional CO poisoning killed about 28,900 people in 2021, nearly 70% of
them men. The global death rate fell by more than half between 2000 and
2021, and the authors call the remaining deaths almost entirely
preventable, through safer heating and cooking devices, cleaner generators
and carbon monoxide alarms.

## Where does carbon monoxide come from at home?

Two autopsy studies from the All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS)
in New Delhi show the pattern clearly. The first looked at 40 deaths between
2010 and 2015: about 80% happened in winter, most often in January, and in
every case but one the victim was in a closed room or space with the source
nearby. The second, covering 56 deaths over fifteen years, found that 95%
happened in winter, that most victims were aged 21 to 30, and that the most
common source was coal burning in earthen or iron vessels used to heat a
room.

The usual sources are:

- **Angithis, sigris and coal or charcoal braziers** brought indoors to warm
  a room, especially when doors and windows are shut for the night.
- **Bukharis and wood or coal stoves** in the hills, where a short or leaky
  chimney lets fumes back into the room, and the **kangri** fire pot in
  Kashmir. NDMA's cold-wave guidelines name these open fire pots directly
  as a cause of CO deaths in closed rooms.
- **LPG gas geysers in bathrooms** without a flue or ventilation (more on
  this below).
- **Cars with the engine running** in a closed garage or basement, or as a
  place to sleep for warmth. The AIIMS series included deaths inside cars
  with the engine on, and NDMA advises never to sleep in a vehicle with the
  engine running.
- **Diesel or petrol generators**, gas stoves and kerosene heaters used in
  small, closed rooms, shops, tents or site huts.
- **House fires.** Smoke from any fire is full of CO. An electric heater does
  not burn fuel and so does not make CO itself, but one left near a quilt
  can start a fire.

## The bathroom faint: gas geyser syndrome

Neurologists have a name for a pattern they see every winter:
**gas geyser syndrome**. A person, often young and healthy, collapses or
has a fit while bathing in a small, closed bathroom with an LPG geyser
running.

At Sir Ganga Ram Hospital in New Delhi, doctors documented 26 such cases
over two years. Eleven looked like seizures, 13 were CO poisoning (four
of them close to cardiac arrest), and two triggered epilepsy. On follow-up,
four of the CO patients had subtle problems with thinking and two developed
early features of Parkinsonism. At MS Ramaiah Medical College in Bengaluru,
a review of 24 people who lost consciousness while bathing with a gas
geyser found that 21 recovered on their own, two were found dead in the
bathroom and one died within a week.

Because the person wakes up and seems fine, these episodes are easy to
mistake for a simple faint or for epilepsy. A 2025 report in the *Indian
Journal of Public Health* again called for strict installation rules and
proper ventilation. The gas distributor Maharashtra Natural Gas Limited is
blunt in its installation guideline: **no gas geyser is permitted in a
bathroom or a concealed area**, because fumes collect and can cause
suffocation.

## Myths and facts about carbon monoxide

**1. Myth: "If there were gas in the room, I'd smell it."**

Fact: CO has no smell. The smell of smoke, or the added smell of LPG, comes
from other things. A clean-burning coal bed or a geyser with poor airflow
can make CO with nothing to smell.

**2. Myth: "Once the coals stop smoking, the angithi is safe to keep in the
bedroom."**

Fact: Glowing coals are still burning, and still need oxygen. Whatever burns
in a closed room is a risk until it is fully out and outside.

**3. Myth: "A bucket of water next to the angithi keeps the air safe."**

Fact: This advice is common, and even shows up in official quotes. A bowl of
water may ease dry air, but water does not soak up carbon monoxide.
Ventilation, and not sleeping with a fire in the room, are what protect you.

**4. Partly true: "Keeping a window open is enough."**

Fact: Ventilation helps, and NDMA and fire officials advise it. But you
cannot judge how much fresh air is enough, and wind and closed doors change
it through the night. Treat an open window as a minimum, not a guarantee.

**5. Myth: "The pulse oximeter reads 98%, so their oxygen is fine."**

Fact: An ordinary fingertip oximeter cannot tell CO from oxygen in the blood.
In a study of 124 poisoned patients, oximeters read an average of 99%
while the true oxygen level in the blood averaged under 89%. A normal reading
proves nothing in suspected CO poisoning.

**6. Myth: "She woke up and feels fine, so there's no need to see a doctor."**

Fact: Some people develop problems with memory, mood or movement days or
weeks later. Anyone who fainted, was confused or was hard to wake needs a
hospital check.

**7. Myth: "It only happens to poor families in villages."**

Fact: The AIIMS victims were mostly young adults in a big city, and gas
geyser cases have been reported from homes in Delhi and Bengaluru. Any home
that burns fuel indoors is at risk.

## Who is most at risk?

Everyone in a closed room breathes the same air, but the CDC says some
people are harmed sooner or more badly:

- **babies and young children;**
- **older adults;**
- **people with heart disease, anaemia or breathing problems** such as
  asthma or COPD;
- **pregnant women**, because the unborn baby is especially vulnerable.
  CDC's clinical guidance tells doctors to test for pregnancy in any woman
  of childbearing age with suspected CO poisoning, and to have a lower
  threshold for intensive treatment;
- **anyone asleep, drunk or sedated**, who will not notice the warning signs.

## What are the early signs?

The symptoms are easy to dismiss, which is why they matter. NDMA, the NHS
and the CDC list:

- headache, often the first sign;
- dizziness, weakness and tiredness;
- nausea or vomiting;
- confusion, drowsiness or blurred vision;
- shortness of breath or chest pain;
- ringing in the ears;
- in severe poisoning, fainting, fits and loss of consciousness.

You may have read that CO turns the lips "cherry red". NDMA lists bright red
lips, and pathologists describe a cherry-pink colour at autopsy, but in
living people it is often absent, and the NHS notes that flushing is harder
to see on brown skin. **Do not wait for a colour
change.**

The strongest clues come from the situation, not the symptom:

- **more than one person, or a pet, feels ill at the same time;**
- **the symptoms started after a heater, angithi, geyser or engine was
  turned on;**
- **there is no fever,** unlike flu;
- **people feel better when they go outside,** and worse when they come
  back in. The NHS lists this as a typical sign.

## What to do in the first minutes

If you suspect carbon monoxide, act on the suspicion. You do not need
proof.

1. **Get everyone out into fresh air, now.** Carry out anyone who is drowsy.
   Open doors and windows on the way if you can do so quickly, but do not
   stay inside to do it.
2. **Turn off the source only if it is quick and safe:** switch off the
   geyser or the gas valve, or the car engine. Do not stay in the room to
   put out an angithi.
3. **Call 112** (or 108 for an ambulance) if anyone is unconscious, confused,
   breathless, has chest pain or had a fit. NDMA advises giving rescue
   breaths if the person is not breathing; if you know CPR, start it.
4. **Lay an unconscious person who is breathing on their side** and stay
   with them until help arrives.
5. **Go to a hospital even if symptoms fade.** Tell the doctors it may be
   carbon monoxide, so they can test the blood level.
6. **Do not go back in** or reuse the heater or geyser until the cause has
   been found and fixed.

## What happens in hospital

The main treatment is oxygen. CDC's clinical guidance advises 100% oxygen
through a mask, usually for about four to five hours or until the
person has no symptoms, along with checks of the heart and nervous system.
The diagnosis is made with a blood test for carboxyhaemoglobin, the form of
haemoglobin that is carrying CO instead of oxygen, since a normal fingertip
oximeter cannot detect it.

Some patients with severe poisoning, unconsciousness or pregnancy may be
considered for hyperbaric oxygen, which means breathing oxygen in a
pressurised chamber. In one US trial, 25% of patients treated this way had
thinking problems at six weeks, compared with 46% of those given ordinary
oxygen. A Cochrane review of all the trials found the evidence too mixed to
be sure of the benefit, and not every hospital has a chamber. Your
doctors will decide what is right.

CDC advises a repeat check about two weeks after discharge, and families
should watch for new forgetfulness, personality change, poor concentration,
trouble walking or stiffness in the weeks after poisoning.

## How to keep your home safe this winter

**Heating**

- **Never sleep in a room with a burning angithi, sigri, kangri, bukhari,
  charcoal stove or kerosene heater.** Use it while everyone is awake, put it
  out completely, and take it outside before bed.
- **Keep a window or ventilator open whenever anything burns indoors,** and
  never block air bricks or vents to keep the cold out.
- **Check bukhari and stove chimneys** before the season, so they are long
  enough, unblocked and lead fully outside.
- **Prefer electric heating where you can.** An electric heater, oil-filled
  radiator or convector makes no CO, but keep it well away from quilts,
  curtains and clothes, and switch it off when you sleep. NDMA advises
  turning heaters off when they are not needed.

**Gas geysers**

- **Install the geyser outside the bathroom,** in a ventilated area, with a
  flue to the outside where possible. The MNGL guideline lists the safety
  features to look for: compliance with IS 15558, a flame failure device and
  an oxygen depletion sensor.
- **If the geyser is already in the bathroom,** fill a bucket first, switch
  the geyser off, and then close the door and bathe. Keep the exhaust fan on
  and the ventilator open.
- **Watch the flame.** MNGL says a flame that turns from blue to yellow with
  black smoke needs a service visit straight away. It also advises
  servicing the burner every six months and replacing the rubber gas tube
  every year.
- **Never let a child bathe alone** in a bathroom with a gas geyser running.

**Cars and generators**

- **Never sleep in a car with the engine running,** and never run an engine
  in a closed garage or basement.
- **Keep generators outdoors,** well away from doors and windows.

**Alarms**

- **Fit a carbon monoxide alarm** in every room with a fuel-burning
  appliance, as the NHS advises, and in the room where people sleep. Test
  it regularly and replace the battery when it warns you. The authors of the
  global study call for alarms to be mandatory for good reason, but an
  alarm is a backstop, not permission to sleep beside a fire.

**Smog is not a reason to seal the room**

Many families shut every window in winter because of smog. Our [guide to
the air pollution season](/blog/air-pollution-season-health-guide) covers
how to cope with bad outdoor air, but a sealed room with a fire in it is far
more dangerous than a little outside air.

## Red flags: get help immediately

**Call 112 or go to the nearest emergency department straight away if,
after time near a heater, angithi, geyser or running engine, someone:**

- is unconscious, very drowsy or cannot be woken properly;
- has had a fit, or has fainted in the bathroom;
- is confused, slurring words or behaving strangely;
- has chest pain, a racing heartbeat or is breathless;
- is pregnant, a baby or an older adult with any symptoms at all.

**See a doctor the same day** if several people in the house have had
headaches, dizziness or nausea that ease when they go outside, even if they
now feel better. Then find the source before anyone sleeps in that room
again.

## The bottom line

Carbon monoxide gives no warning you can see or smell, and in
winter it kills people in their sleep, most often young, healthy adults in
closed rooms with an angithi or a coal heater. The fix costs almost nothing:
never sleep with anything burning in the room, keep gas geysers out of
closed bathrooms, never sit in a running car in a closed space, and consider
a carbon monoxide alarm. If anyone gets a headache, dizziness or confusion
near a fire or a geyser, get everyone into fresh air first and ask questions
later.

*This article is general health information, not medical advice. If you
think someone has carbon monoxide poisoning, get them into fresh air and
call 112 or go to the nearest hospital.*
